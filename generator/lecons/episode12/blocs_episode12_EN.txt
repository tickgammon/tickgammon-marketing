Hello and welcome to The Owl's Lessons! Last time, you travelled back in time with the history of backgammon.

Today is the last lesson of the season: the player's vocabulary. Blot, prime, gammon, doubling cube… By the end, you'll talk backgammon like a club regular! Let's go!

Let's start with the board. Each of the twenty-four triangles is called a point.

The distance from one point to the next is called a pip. A checker that moves five points moves five pips.

In the middle, the central strip is called the bar. Your points one to six are your home board, and your points seven to twelve, your outer board.

A single checker on a point is called a blot. It's fragile: your opponent can hit it.

With at least two checkers, you make a point. It's yours: your opponent can no longer land there.

And a point you hold in your opponent's home board is called an anchor. It's a safe haven, deep in their camp.

To slow your opponent down, you block. Your seven-point, right next to the bar, is called the bar point.

Several made points in a row form a prime. With six points in a row, the prime is complete: nobody can get past it!

And a checker placed a few pips away from a point you want to make is called a builder. It prepares your next point.

When your checker lands on an opponent's blot, you hit it: the hit checker goes to the bar.

That checker must then enter from the bar into its opponent's home board, before anything else can be played.

A blot six pips away or less can be hit with a single die: that's a direct shot. Further away, you have to add both dice: that's an indirect shot, much less likely.

When no checker can pass the opponent's checkers anymore, the game becomes a race.

To know who's ahead, you do a pip count: the total distance your checkers still have to travel. At the start, it's one hundred and sixty-seven for each player.

When all fifteen of your checkers are in your home board, you can bear them off. The first player to bear off all of them wins the game.

Here is the doubling cube, the big die marked two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two and sixty-four. Players often just call it the cube.

Before rolling the dice, you can double: that means offering to double the value of the game.

Your opponent then chooses. Either they take, and the game goes on, worth twice as much. Or they pass, and they lose the game straight away, at its current value.

Winning normally is a single game: it counts for its basic value.

If the loser hasn't borne off any checker, it's a gammon: the win counts double.

And if they also still have a checker on the bar or in the winner's home board, it's a backgammon: the win counts triple! [laughs] So the game is named after the worst possible defeat.

In clubs and tournaments, people play matches: a series of games to a number of points set in advance, for example five or seven points.

When a player gets to one point from victory, the next game is played without the doubling cube: that's the Crawford rule.

The very first move of the game is called the opening. There are only fifteen of them, and you discovered them in lesson two.

And the chouette? It's backgammon for three or more players: the box plays alone against the whole team, led by a captain. You can guess why it's my favourite game!

And there you go! Point, blot, anchor, prime, pip count, doubling cube, gammon… you now know the essential words of the player.
